The principle behind the tool

Treat outside capital as your last resort, and climb the ladder one rung at a time.

Every rung costs more than the one before it, in money, control or obligations. A founder should use up the cheaper rungs first. Business size also decides which rungs are realistic, because lenders, grant makers and investors each set requirements that very small businesses cannot meet.

Before you raise

Fundraising carries four truths that founders often learn too late.

The money does not belong to you.

Every naira you raise comes with a lender or an investor who expects it back, with interest or with a share of what you build. You hold it on their behalf.

Raising money will not make you rich.

The most likely result of a raise is more debt and more responsibility. Wealth comes later, if the work that follows the raise goes well.

The raise starts the hard part.

Reporting, repayments and higher targets arrive with the money. The stress goes up, and the journey only begins on the day the funds land.

Debt is usually the most straightforward outside cash.

Grants and equity each come with their own conditions, long timelines and competition. When a business is ready for outside cash, a loan that it can repay is often the most direct route.

Raising money is also not a badge of achievement. A founder who grows steadily from revenue has achieved as much as a founder who announces a round, and often keeps more of the business.

Ask these questions first

You can often get what you need without raising cash.

Customers

Raise money from your own clients.

Ask for deposits, take preorders or offer a discount for payment upfront. Your customers can fund the next batch of work.

Sales

Pay someone a commission to sell for you.

A good sales agent who earns only when they sell can bring in more cash than a loan, and costs you nothing until the money arrives.

Cash flow

Collect what customers already owe.

Money stuck with debtors is money you already earned. Tighter payment terms can release cash faster than any loan.

Inventory

Ask your supplier for credit.

A trader who needs stock can negotiate a line of credit with a supplier, take goods now and pay after the goods sell.

Services

Negotiate terms with your service providers.

Landlords, logistics firms and other providers will sometimes accept staged payments or a share of revenue instead of cash upfront.

Machinery

Rent equipment that sits idle elsewhere.

Another company may own similar machines that it underuses. A rental agreement lets you learn how much you truly need before you buy.

Competitors

Collaborate with a business you compete with.

Two similar businesses can share a delivery van, buy stock together for a better price or split a large order that neither could fill alone.

Logistics

Share the load with a partner.

A partnership for distribution, storage or transport can remove a cost that you would otherwise borrow to cover.

A story from the field

A cheap loan solved the wrong problem for one food business.

During COVID, I helped a small food processing business apply for an equipment loan. The interest rate was below ten percent, which looked excellent on paper. The loan paid for dryers and other machinery.

Cash flow was already tight. Customers owed the business around ₦500,000 in unpaid invoices, and the new repayments squeezed what little cash remained. Looking back, the business needed restructuring far more than it needed machines.

What the loan bought

The loan bought new dryers and processing machinery, which increased capacity that the business could not yet sell.

What would have helped more

The founder needed to prove the product, set the right price, collect debts and open new retail outlets. If cash was unavoidable, a small delivery vehicle would have served her far better.
